Reaction Time Test: Measure Your Visual Reflex Speed

Visual reaction time is the delay between seeing a signal and acting on it. On a simple on-screen test, healthy adults commonly land between 200 and 270 milliseconds, although your screen, browser and input device add a little delay of their own.

How to use it

  1. Tap the pad to arm the test. It turns red while you wait.
  2. Watch for green. The wait is random, between roughly 1.5 and 4 seconds, so you cannot anticipate it.
  3. Tap as fast as you can. Your time appears in milliseconds, and tapping early restarts the round.
  4. Repeat at least five times. Your average uses your latest ten attempts; your personal best is saved on this device.

Why it works

When the pad changes colour, light is converted to electrical signals in the retina, travels along the optic nerve to the visual cortex, and is passed to the motor cortex, which sends the command to your finger. Each step takes time, which is why even a fast human response takes a fifth of a second or more.

Tips for consistent results

  • Test at the same time of day and on the same device so results are comparable.
  • Judge yourself by your average, not by one lucky tap.
  • Sleep, hydration, caffeine and mental fatigue all shift reaction speed from day to day.
  • A high-refresh-rate display and a wired mouse can shave off a few milliseconds.

Common questions

Is a 300 ms reaction time bad?

Not necessarily. Phones, touchscreens and wireless devices add delay, and tiredness slows everyone down. Compare against your own baseline rather than a single number.

Why does it say “Too early”?

The delay before green is random on purpose. Tapping before the signal means you guessed, so the round resets.

Can I improve my reaction time?

Practice, good sleep and a focused state help on this kind of task, though gains tend to level off once you reach your personal ceiling.

Zen Pomodoro Timer: Science-Backed Work & Rest Intervals

The Pomodoro Technique is a classic time-boxing strategy developed by Francesco Cirillo in the late 1980s. By breaking work into focused 25-minute sprints separated by short rest periods, you reduce mental friction, prevent burnout, and build consistent momentum.

How to use it

  1. Choose a single task to work on without multi-tasking.
  2. Click Start Session to begin a 25-minute Focus interval.
  3. Work uninterrupted until you hear the soft acoustic completion chime.
  4. Take a 5-minute Short Break to stretch, hydrate, or breathe.
  5. After completing 4 focus sessions, take a longer 15-minute Long Break to fully reset.

Why it works for deep focus

Human attention naturally ebbs and flows. Prolonged focus without rest leads to decision fatigue and cognitive drift. Time-boxing creates a clear boundary that signals to your brain that exertion is temporary, making it easier to overcome procrastination.

Mindful productivity tips

  • Turn off non-essential notifications during your 25-minute focus blocks.
  • Step away from your screen during 5-minute breaks—rest your eyes by looking at distant objects.
  • If an intrusive thought pops up during work, use the Thought Dissolver or write it down for later.
  • Combine break periods with 1–2 minutes of Box Breathing to lower stress levels.

Common questions

What if I get interrupted during a focus block?

If possible, pause or note the distraction and return to your task immediately. If the interruption is major, reset the timer and start fresh when ready.

Can I customize the timer durations?

Yes. The Zen Pomodoro tool includes presets for standard 25-minute focus blocks, 5-minute short breaks, and 15-minute long recovery breaks.

Is there an alarm sound when the timer finishes?

Instead of a loud or jarring alarm, our Web Audio synthesizer plays a gentle acoustic chime to peacefully inform you when a session ends.

Schulte Table Online: 5×5 Focus & Visual Scanning Trainer

A Schulte table is a grid of shuffled numbers that you find in order, from 1 to 25. Associated with the German psychiatrist Walter Schulte, it has long been used in attention research, sports psychology and speed-reading courses.

How to use it

  1. Rest your gaze on the centre of the grid.
  2. Tap 1, then 2, then 3, and so on up to 25. The timer starts on your first correct tap.
  3. Use your peripheral vision to spot the next number instead of scanning row by row.
  4. Press “New Random Grid” for a fresh layout and try to beat your fastest time.

Why it works

Each round asks you to hold the next target in mind, search a cluttered field, and ignore distractions, which exercises visual search, selective attention and attention switching. Because the numbers are reshuffled every time, you cannot memorise positions, so improvement reflects better scanning rather than recall.

Research on whether Schulte tables directly improve reading speed is limited, so treat them as a focus warm-up rather than a guaranteed speed-reading fix.

Practice ideas

  • Do three or four grids as a short focus warm-up before study or deep work.
  • Track your own best time; speeds vary widely between people and devices.
  • Try a “soft focus” approach: relax your eyes and let numbers pop out instead of hunting.
  • Stop if your eyes feel strained, and look into the distance for a few seconds.

Common questions

How long should a Schulte grid take?

There is no official standard. Beginners are often slower, and times drop with practice. Compare against your own fastest time.

Does it improve speed reading?

It trains visual scanning and attention, which support reading, but evidence for large reading-speed gains is limited.

How often should I practise?

A few grids once or twice a day is plenty. Short, regular sessions beat one long one.

Guided Breathing Exercises: Box, 4-7-8 & Resonance Breathing

Slow, paced breathing is one of the quickest ways to settle a racing heart. This guided breathing tool uses an expanding orb so you can follow the rhythm without counting.

How to use it

  1. Pick a pattern: Box 4-4-4-4, Sleep 4-7-8 or Calm 5-5.
  2. Press “Start Breathing Session” and sit comfortably with your shoulders relaxed.
  3. Inhale as the orb grows, hold when it pauses, and exhale as it shrinks.
  4. Continue for three to five minutes, then press the button again to stop.

Why it works

Slow breathing with long exhales increases vagal activity and heart rate variability (HRV), nudging your body from fight-or-flight toward rest-and-digest. That is why paced breathing is widely used for stress management, pre-performance nerves and winding down at night.

  • Box breathing (4-4-4-4): equal inhale, hold, exhale and hold. Popular for steady focus under pressure.
  • 4-7-8 breathing: a long exhale pattern popularised by Dr. Andrew Weil, often used before sleep.
  • Calm 5-5: about six breaths a minute, close to the resonance rate used in HRV training.

When to use each pattern

  • Before a meeting, exam or presentation: Box 4-4-4-4.
  • In bed when your mind will not switch off: 4-7-8.
  • For a gentle reset during the day: Calm 5-5.
  • Breathe through your nose if comfortable, and keep the breath smooth rather than forced.

Common questions

How long should I breathe with the orb?

Three to five minutes is a good start. Even one minute can help you feel calmer.

I feel dizzy. What should I do?

Stop, return to normal breathing and rest. Breath-holds may not suit everyone, so check with a clinician if you have heart, lung or pregnancy-related concerns.

Can breathing exercises replace therapy?

No. They are a self-help skill that can sit alongside professional care for anxiety, panic or sleep problems.

    Stress Relief Micro-Tasks & Thought Shredder

    When stress builds, big solutions feel out of reach. Tiny physical actions and putting your worries into words can lower the pressure in a couple of minutes.

    How to use it

    Why it works

    Writing a worry down moves it from working memory onto the screen, a process called cognitive offloading.     Brain Training Games: Sequence Memory, Stroop Test & Number Span

    Three quick cognitive games that challenge working memory, attention control and mental flexibility. Each takes about a minute, which makes them easy to fit between tasks.

    How to use it

    1. Pick a game from the tabs at the top of the tool.
    2. Sequence Memory: watch the pads light up, then repeat the pattern. Each level adds a step.
    3. Color Focus (Stroop): choose the ink colour of the word, ignoring what the word says.
    4. Number Span: memorise the digits shown, then type them back; the number grows as you succeed.

    Why it works

    Working memory holds a few items in mind at once; classic research suggested about seven, and newer estimates point nearer four chunks. The Stroop effect, first described by John Ridley Stroop in 1935, shows how automatic reading interferes with naming colours, so beating it exercises inhibitory control.

    Brain games reliably improve performance on the games themselves. Whether that carries over to everyday thinking is still debated, so enjoy them as a warm-up and keep other healthy habits too.

    Getting better results

    • Play when you are alert; tired scores are not representative.
    • Chunk digits into groups (for example 3-3-2) to extend your number span.
    • For Stroop, say the colour silently and look slightly past the word.
    • Quit while it is still fun; short sessions work best.

    Common questions

    Do brain games make you smarter?

    They can improve the specific skills you practise. Broader gains are not firmly established.

    Which game is best for focus?

    The Stroop game puts the most pressure on attention control, while Sequence Memory targets working memory.

    Are my scores saved?

    Best scores are stored locally on your device only.

    5-4-3-2-1 Grounding Exercise: Calm Racing Thoughts in Minutes

    The 5-4-3-2-1 technique is a well-known grounding exercise that uses your five senses to bring attention out of worry and into the present moment. This guide walks you through it one step at a time.

    How to use it

    1. Press “Start Step 1” and follow the prompt.
    2. Notice 5 things you can see, 4 you can feel, 3 you can hear, 2 you can smell and 1 you can taste.
    3. Say each item quietly or in your head, and take your time.
    4. Finish with one slow exhale before returning to your day.

    Why it works

    Anxious thoughts usually live in the past or future. Naming what is physically around you draws on sensory attention, which competes with rumination and helps the nervous system settle. Grounding skills like this are common in cognitive-behavioural and trauma-informed approaches.

    When it helps most

    • Before a meeting, call or exam when your mind is racing.
    • When you notice spiralling “what if” thinking.
    • During a stressful commute or while waiting.
    • Alongside box breathing for a fuller reset.

    Common questions

    How long does it take?

    Usually two to five minutes. Go slower if you want a deeper reset.

    What if I cannot smell or taste anything?

    Imagine a favourite scent or flavour, or notice the taste in your mouth. Adapt the steps to what works for you.

    What if my anxiety does not ease?

    Repeat the exercise or try a breathing pattern. If you feel panicked or unsafe, reach out to a professional or emergency services.
